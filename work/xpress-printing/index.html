Xpress Printing 24/7 — ordering and quoting platform

A print shop was running on a WordPress brochure site whose price list had been uploaded as images in 2020. Every job started with a phone call, and too many of them ended in a pricing dispute. I replaced it with a 220-product catalogue that quotes itself — then handed over every password.

The situation

The shop had a website, which is not the same thing as having anything online that works. It was a WordPress brochure site, and the price list on it was a set of images uploaded in 2020 — so the prices were both out of date and impossible to search, read on a phone, or calculate anything from.

The practical effect was that the website generated phone calls rather than orders. Someone would ring, describe a job, get a price read out to them, and go away to think about it. And because the quote lived in a conversation rather than a record, too many jobs ended in a pricing dispute at the counter — the customer remembered one number, the shop had quoted another, and neither had anything to point at.

What I built

  • A 220+ product catalogue with live quoting. The price updates as the customer selects size, quantity and finish. The quote is produced by the same logic every time, which is what actually removes the disputes — not a nicer-looking page.
  • Guest checkout. No account required. A print customer ordering business cards once is not going to create a password to do it.
  • An admin dashboard covering the full order workflow, usable from a phone. This one mattered more than it sounds — the people running the shop are on the floor, not at a desk.
  • Customer order history and automated status emails sent from the business's own domain, so the shop's messages arrive looking like the shop rather than like a third-party tool.

Decisions and trade-offs

I deliberately did not build online payments

This was the obvious feature to add, and the client would probably have accepted it. I left it out because it doesn't match how the shop actually operates: they invoice — a deposit to start the job, the balance at pickup. Bolting a payment processor onto that would have created a second, parallel way for money to arrive, and someone would have had to reconcile the two.

The trade: it's a less impressive feature list. Online payments would have been a feature for my portfolio, not for the client. I build to the operation, not to a template.

Full ownership handover, from day one

The domain, source code, database and customer records all sit in accounts registered to the client, not to me. No retainer, no licence, no dependency. If we never speak again, nothing stops working.

The trade: it gives up the recurring revenue most agencies build into this kind of project. It also removes the single biggest reason a small business hesitates to commission custom software — the fear of being locked to whoever built it.

Managed infrastructure rather than assembling a stack

I used proven managed services rather than standing up and maintaining every piece myself, and leaned on AI for the mechanical parts of the build. That's what made four weeks possible instead of four months.

The trade: less control over the lower layers than the self-hosted approach I took on Kirana. For a business that needs the thing working and cheap to run rather than infinitely tunable, that's the right way round.

Outcome

The site is live and the shop runs its orders through it. The business owns every part of it outright, and the monthly running cost is close to nothing.

I don't have order-volume figures to show — that's the client's data, and I'd need their permission before publishing any of it. What I can say is what changed structurally: a quote is now a record the customer and the shop are both looking at, rather than a number remembered from a phone call.
